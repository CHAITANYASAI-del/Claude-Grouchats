Claude Groupchats

Claude's biggest use case is teamwork.Claude listens to one person at a time.

About 80% of Anthropic's revenue comes from teams and companies. But two people still can't work in the same Claude chat at the same time. This is the case for fixing that: the people already asking for it, why the numbers work for Claude even though they didn't for ChatGPT, and the working version I built to find out.

The whole argument, in five steps

  1. 01 — The business

    Anthropic gets paid per person on a team.

    $20 a seat, minimum 20 seats. About 80% of revenue comes from company accounts, not individuals. More people using it means more money.

  2. 02 — The problem

    But only one person can talk to Claude — so everyone else's expertise has to go through their keyboard.

    Real work has parts, and different people are the expert on different parts. The person who owns the database, or the design direction, or the failing service can't ask their own question — they have to describe it to whoever is typing. Every handoff loses something, and starting a fresh chat throws away the context already built.

  3. 03 — The evidence

    The request is already filed on Anthropic's GitHub — and it wasn't the first time.

    Issue #48828 on anthropics/claude-code asks for a shareable session link and multi-user chat. Anthropic's own triage closed it as a duplicate, meaning someone had filed the same request before it. The Claude community on Reddit has asked for it too. And of the eight people I interviewed — an SDE 3 at Amazon, a data analyst at the City of Chicago, a senior product designer, engineering graduates — every single one had already built themselves a workaround.

  4. 04 — The catch

    OpenAI tried this and shut it down. But their prices worked differently.

    ChatGPT group chats were a social feature on a flat monthly fee, so every extra person cost OpenAI money. When you charge per person instead, adding people earns money rather than losing it. That one difference is the whole case.

  5. 05 — What I did

    I built it, tested it on real work, and wrote up how Claude could ship this properly.

    This is a case study, not a pitch. I built a working Chrome extension and put it in front of real teams for sessions lasting up to three hours — then used what I learned to set out what a native version inside Claude should look like, how it should roll out, and what would have to be true for it to work.

Where the product stops short

Coding is what people use Claude for most — about 35% of chats on Claude.ai and 44% of API traffic, according to Anthropic's own Economic Index. And coding is almost always shared work: the same codebase, the same outage, the same spec, worked on by more than one person.

But right now a second person can't join someone else's live Claude chat. So every time a team works together, it turns into one person typing while the others watch — or they each open their own chat, and each one starts from nothing.

There is a gap between one person using Claude and a whole team running on it. Today the product has no way to close it.

Why hasn't Anthropic built this already?

Not because it's a bad idea. Anthropic's engineers have been busy elsewhere: making the model smarter, building Claude Code, and teaching Claude to use tools and run jobs on its own. When they have worked on "more than one thing at once," they meant several AI agents working together — not several people. Letting humans share a chat sits right next to that work, but it isn't the same job, so it hasn't come up yet.

That's the useful kind of gap to look for. Not "what is this company bad at," but "what have they simply not got to yet."

There's also a precedent inside the product already. In Claude Code, several AI agents share one context and work on the same task. Opening that up to a second person — someone the account owner deliberately invited — is a smaller step than it sounds.

03

Who this is for

Three kinds of user came out of the interviews, and they all described the same shape of problem. A job has several parts, different people are the expert on different parts, and only one of them can actually talk to Claude.

The problem isn't that people can't watch. It's that the person who knows the answer has to dictate it to whoever is holding the keyboard.

That's the mental model worth holding onto. Somebody starts a piece of work and gets it as far as their own expertise goes. Then it hits the database, or the design direction, or the part someone else owns — and right now the only way forward is to describe the problem to a colleague and retype their answer. Every handoff loses something, and the person best placed to ask the next question is the one who can't ask it.

All three would buy for different reasons, and only the first is a real revenue case today.

04

OpenAI shipped this and killed it. That is the objection to beat.

Any serious version of this proposal has to survive the fact that the closest competitor tried multiplayer AI, scaled it globally, and retired it inside eight months.

November 2025
ChatGPT group chats launch
Up to 20 people in one conversation with the AI. Piloted in four countries, expanded worldwide within a week on the strength of early feedback.
July 9, 2026
OpenAI begins retiring it
No new group chats, no converting existing chats, no joining by invite link. Existing group chats become read-only. OpenAI's stated reason: "simplifying the experience."
Through 2026
Google ships sharing, not sessions
Gemini added conversation sharing as a link — a static snapshot, like a read-only doc. No live, joint session where two people type into the same conversation.

What OpenAI actually tested — and what they didn't

OpenAI tried multiplayer at the level of casual conversation: plan a dinner, settle an argument, share a joke. The AI was one more voice in a social group chat. That is a hard place to win. People already have somewhere to talk to their friends, and unlike a messaging app, a chatbot doesn't get more useful just because more people joined it.

But look carefully at what that experiment never tested. It didn't ask whether several people want an AI inside a shared piece of work — one codebase, one bug, one document, with something concrete being produced at the end. Chatting together and working together are different jobs. Failing at the first tells you very little about the second.

There were real privacy problems too. By default, anything said in a group chat could be used to train OpenAI's models, and anyone holding the invite link could read the whole history.

Where I'd push back on my own argument

That's a tidy explanation, and tidy explanations after the fact are worth doubting. OpenAI never published its usage or safety data, so here are three other reasons it could have been shut down — none of them about chatting versus working.

People stopped coming back. Group chats may simply have lost their novelty. That's testable, which is why section 09 tracks repeat use over weeks rather than sign-ups.

Rooms of people are expensive to police. One participant steering a shared AI in front of everyone else is a real safety surface at global consumer scale. Section 08 covers why that surface is smaller inside a company.

Compute went somewhere more valuable. This is the one I find most interesting. Through 2026 OpenAI pushed Codex into every ChatGPT plan, and agentic coding work costs far more compute per user than ordinary chat. Cutting a low-value social feature to free capacity for a high-value coding one is a perfectly rational decision that says nothing about whether multiplayer works. And if that is what happened, it strengthens the case here rather than weakening it — because it would mean group chats were killed for costing money, and the next section is about why, on per-seat pricing, they don't.

The first two objections were raised by Ruslan Musayev on LinkedIn. All three are hypotheses — OpenAI hasn't said.

05

On a flat monthly price, every extra person costs the company money. When you charge per person, it works the other way round.

This is the part the "wrong positioning" argument can't explain on its own. Group chats didn't only fail OpenAI on framing. They failed on money — and the reason is how the product was priced.

Every time you send a message, it costs the company real money to run the AI. On a flat $20-a-month plan, that cost goes up the more you use it, but the price you pay stays the same. So the heaviest users are already the least profitable ones. Put three people in one conversation and you've tripled the cost against the same fixed fee. The feature loses more money the better it works.

Claude's business charges the opposite way. Companies pay $20 per person per month, with a minimum of 20 people, and about 80% of revenue comes from those accounts. When a team uses Claude more, they're using something they've already paid for. So the question isn't "does this cost us more?" It's "does it cost more than those same three people working in three separate chats?"

It doesn't. It costs a little less.

Cost to reach the same answer: three separate chats vs. one shared chat
Effective input tokens processed. Three people, one problem, about an hour. Modelled — assumptions below.
Duplicated cold-start context Cached prefix reads New prompt tokens

~14% cheaper to serve — and it reaches the answer in 30 prompts instead of roughly 40, because nobody repeats work they couldn't see.

Assumptions, so you can break them

  • Three people, one problem, about an hour, 30 useful messages in total.
  • Explaining the problem at the start (the code, the error, what's been tried) costs about 4,000 tokens. A token is roughly three-quarters of a word.
  • Each message after that adds about 400 new tokens.
  • Re-reading a conversation you've already sent costs about 10% of the price of new text. (This is called caching — the system remembers what it already read.)
  • Working in separate chats, nobody sees anyone else's questions, so about 35% of the work gets repeated: 40 messages instead of 30.
  • Every separate chat has to explain the problem from scratch: 3 × 4,000 instead of 1 × 4,000.

Change any of those numbers and the gap moves. If people somehow never repeated each other's work, the shared chat would actually be slightly more expensive, because one long conversation costs more to carry than three short ones. That's the honest weak point in this argument, so it's worth saying plainly: the saving comes from people not repeating each other, not from anything magic about sharing.

The economics don't say "build this because it's cheap." They say "the thing that killed it at OpenAI doesn't apply here."

Where the revenue actually comes from

Not from the feature itself. From the extra seats it sells. Anthropic already has more than 300,000 business customers paying per person. If even a cautious 25% of them have two or more people working on the same things, that's around 75,000 accounts that could buy more seats — without a salesperson having to find a single new customer.

Other companies have already proven this pattern. Figma let people collaborate for free and charged for files instead, and 70% of its biggest customers started on a cheaper plan. Dropbox's "invite a friend" loop brought in about 35% of its sign-ups. Slack grew because people dragged their teammates in, and paying became the obvious next step.

If shared sessions lifted seat purchases by even 5–10% — far less than Figma saw — that compounds across customers Anthropic already has. That's a guess, not a forecast. And the cheapest way to check a guess isn't to write a longer document. It's to build the thing and watch what people do with it.

The Jevons risk, stated up front

Make something easier and people don't use the same amount of it — they use much more. That's the goal when you're paid per person. It only becomes a problem if the cost of running one team's chats grows faster than what that team pays. The safeguard is that shared sessions draw on the capacity an account has already bought, rather than opening a new bucket nobody is paying for. That's why the rollout starts with teams already on Claude Code, not with free accounts.

07

What I built

The owner installs a Chrome extension and shares a link. A teammate opens that link in a plain web page — no install — and can read the live transcript and send prompts that land inside the owner's real Claude session. Responses mirror back to everyone instantly.

The decisions that mattered

DecisionWhat I choseWhy
Where to build itChrome extension, not a desktop appClaude's desktop app only works locally. The browser exposes DOM and content scripts with zero system permissions, and Claude's web app already runs on cloud sync — making the browser the only frictionless, zero-install entry point for the joining teammate.
How to relay messagesCloudflare Workers + Durable ObjectsThe only option meeting all four constraints at once: instant (native WebSockets), free, never sleeps (the Hibernation API keeps idle sockets alive unbilled), and no credit card. One Durable Object per session gives each conversation a strongly consistent, isolated room.
How to identify a sessionToken derived deterministically from the claude.ai conversation ID, not a random codeSame conversation → same token → same Durable Object → persistent history and remembered participants, even across re-shares and days later.

The hardest technical problem

Claude has no public API for this, and claude.ai virtualizes the DOM — off-screen messages are removed as you scroll. Everything had to be reconstructed from the rendered page:

  • Scroll-to-scrape — programmatically scroll to the top repeatedly to force lazy-load, then walk top-to-bottom capturing every turn.
  • Position-based dedup — each message is keyed by its absolute vertical position, which stays stable while scrolling, so repeated passes don't create duplicates.
  • Double-render fix — claude.ai renders each user message twice (visible plus an accessibility copy), collapsed into one before display.
  • Live capture — a MutationObserver watches for new Claude replies as they stream in, while teammate prompts are injected into the composer and submitted on their behalf.

Wire protocol

MessageFrom → toEffect
owner_historyextension → DOReplaces stored history with the authoritative scrape (plus last-30s live extras); broadcasts to viewers
owner_sent_messageextension → DOStores and broadcasts the owner's prompt
owner_claude_responseextension → DOStores and broadcasts Claude's reply; dedups identical consecutive replies

The known-bad part

The scraping layer is the highest-risk component in the system, because it depends on claude.ai's current DOM rather than a stable API contract. I treated that as a bounded, deliberate risk: acceptable for a validation build, not acceptable as a permanent feature. It is the single strongest reason the recommendation is to rebuild this natively rather than harden the scraper.

08

The questions reviewers actually asked

These came from engineers and PMs who read the first version of this case study. Every one of them exposed something the original didn't answer.

QWhat happens when multiple people steer the same session toward conflicting goals?

This is the hardest design problem in the whole idea, and it isn't the same as two people editing a document. In Google Docs, two people typing in different paragraphs both just work. A conversation has only one thread — so a second question sent halfway through an answer doesn't sit alongside the first, it changes the subject.

The prototype handles this the simplest honest way: messages go into the owner's chat one at a time, in the order they arrive. Nobody's question is thrown away, but a question sent while Claude is replying lands after that reply, not inside it.

That's fine for a test build and not good enough for a real one. A proper version needs three things this one doesn't have. A visible queue, so you can see your teammate's question is already waiting and don't ask the same thing. Names attached to questions, so Claude knows who asked what and can say "you two want different things" instead of splitting the difference. And a clear rule for taking turns — I'd let people send whenever they want, with a gentle lock while Claude is writing, because forcing people to wait their turn brings back the exact problem this feature exists to remove.

The thing to design against isn't people disagreeing. It's people disagreeing invisibly — your question disappearing into an answer that was already being written.

Raised by Ruslan Musayev, Senior Software Engineer, on LinkedIn.

QIsn't the "social vs. work tool" explanation for OpenAI's retirement too clean?

Yes. It is addressed in full in section 04 — the short version is that retention decay and moderation cost are equally plausible explanations, neither has been ruled out publicly, and both would apply here in weaker form. The measurement plan in section 09 treats repeat use as a leading indicator specifically because of this objection.

Also raised by Ruslan Musayev.

QWhat stops a shared session from becoming a data-leak incident?

Nothing, in the prototype — and that is the honest answer. A shared debugging session can contain credentials, customer data, or internal-only context the owner never meant to broadcast, and a link-based invite is exactly the model that drew criticism at OpenAI.

A native version cannot copy this extension's sharing model. It needs the governance layer Anthropic already sells to Enterprise: SSO-scoped invites so only verified org-domain accounts can join, audit logs of who joined and when, and admin controls to disable sharing entirely for regulated accounts.

And I'd ship that off-switch with the feature, not after it. The tempting order is to release sharing now and add the controls in a follow-up — that's how most features get staged, and usually it's the right call. It isn't here. The moment sharing goes live, any company that should never have had it enabled has already shared something, and a switch added a month later can't un-share what went out. Controls that exist to prevent an incident have to exist before the incident is possible.

So of everything in this proposal, "an admin can turn this off for their whole company" is the one thing I would hold the launch for.

QDoesn't a multi-user room create a moderation surface?

A smaller one than a consumer product does, because the invite graph is bounded by an org domain rather than open to the public, and participants are identified rather than anonymous. Accountability inside a workplace does most of the work that a global consumer rollout has to do with policy tooling. It is still a real surface, and it is a reason to ship org-scoped before anything broader.

10

What I'd bring to the roadmap

Not "ship this extension." Build it natively into Claude Team and Enterprise, and measure it against seat expansion rather than engagement.

The three metrics that decide it

  • Seat-expansion rate for accounts that use shared sessions versus those that don't.
  • Time-to-second-seat after a team's first collaborative session.
  • Retention and renewal lift for multi-seat accounts using the feature versus a matched control.

11

Honest limitations

  • Dependent on claude.ai's DOM. Upstream class-name changes require selector updates — inherent to any unofficial, non-API integration.
  • Coarse timestamps. claude.ai only exposes relative times ("N hours ago"), so ordering is precise but wall-clock precision is not.
  • Storage is per-deployment. History persists in Durable Object storage scoped to the deployed Worker — fine at this stage, would need re-architecting for a sanctioned version.
  • Small n throughout. 8 interviews and a seed group of 12–20. Directional evidence, not measurement.
  • No billing surface. The core commercial hypothesis — seat expansion — is untestable from outside Anthropic. This case study can establish that the need is real and the economics are not hostile; it cannot prove the revenue claim.
  • Built outside Anthropic's infrastructure by necessity. No API existed. The right long-term home is a native, sanctioned feature, not a third-party extension.
